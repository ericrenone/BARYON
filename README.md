## Part I · The RHIC Discovery and Its Immediate Implications

### I.1 The Textbook Crisis

For sixty years, the standard picture of the proton has been as follows: three valence quarks (two up, one down) bound together by gluons. The proton has a baryon number of +1. This baryon number is divided equally among the three quarks: each carries +1/3. The electric charge is distributed similarly: the two up quarks each carry +2/3, the down quark carries −1/3, totaling +1. This distribution — among the three valence quarks — is the complete story taught in every physics textbook since 1960.

On August 16, 2026, the STAR collaboration at Brookhaven National Laboratory published findings in *Science* that demolish this picture.

Using data from the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC), which operated from 2000 to early 2026, the STAR team observed an unexpected excess of baryons emerging perpendicular to the collision axis in high-energy nuclear smashes. The phenomenon had puzzled researchers for years. If baryon number were truly carried by the three valence quarks alone, the excess baryons should only appear when all three quarks were stopped and transformed in the collision — an event that should be rare and should leave a distinctive signature in the electric charge redistribution.

But the observation revealed a striking mismatch. The STAR physicists measured roughly twice as many baryons as the electric-charge signature predicted. If the three valence quarks were the sole carriers of baryon number, this result should not occur. The excess baryons implied an additional carrier — something carrying baryon number that was not accounted for in the quark-only model.

The researchers proposed an explanation: the baryon number is carried not by the quarks themselves but by a Y-shaped structure of gluons that connects the three quarks. This "gluon junction" or "baryon junction" — a concept first proposed theoretically in the 1970s and formalized by Dmitri Kharzeev in 1996 — is easier to stop in a high-energy collision than the three valence quarks moving at relativistic speeds. When the junction is stopped, it converts its energy into new baryons that travel perpendicular to the beam. The valence quarks, meanwhile, continue forward along the beampipe, eventually fragmenting into mesons.

The mechanism is elegant in its simplicity: the junction, being a collective structure of gluons, carries less momentum per unit of information than the individual quarks. At extreme collision energies, when gluons multiply and split within the proton, each individual gluon carries a smaller fraction of the proton's momentum than the three valence quarks do. The junction therefore behaves like a slower-moving target — easier to stop, easier to transform.

### I.2 The Boundary as the Carrier

The crucial insight is this: the baryon number is not a property of the quarks. It is a property of the structure that binds them. The quarks are observable — we can, in principle, track their energies, their momenta, their fragmentation products. But the gluon junction is not directly observable in the same way. It is a virtual structure, a collective excitation that emerges from the quantum chromodynamic (QCD) field. Yet it is precisely this unobservable structure that carries the conserved quantity.

This inversion — the conservation law residing in what cannot be directly measured rather than in what can — is the defining feature of the col(F)/ker(F) partition principle. In the language of functional analysis:

- **col(F)** (column space, or image): the three valence quarks and their direct interactions. This is what we can observe, measure, predict from naive models.
- **ker(F)** (kernel, or null space): the gluon junction. This is invisible to direct measurement, but it is what determines whether the system remains conserved.

The baryon number lives in ker(F). The three quarks live in col(F). Together, they form a partition across a boundary. The boundary IS the carrier of conservation.

### I.3 The Experimental Protocol

The STAR experiment's methodology is instructive. The physicists did not directly measure the gluon junction. Instead, they measured two quantities:

1. **Baryon number excess**: the net count of baryons minus antibaryons appearing perpendicular to the collision axis.
2. **Electric charge redistribution**: how much electric charge was transferred from the forward direction to the perpendicular direction.

The comparison between these two measurements revealed the inconsistency. Since electric charge is carried by the quarks (the gluons are electrically neutral), the charge redistribution tells us how many quarks were stopped and transformed. But the baryon excess was roughly twice as large as this quark-stop rate predicted.

The only explanation: an additional source of baryon number that does not carry electric charge. The gluon junction fits perfectly. It carries baryon number but not electric charge.

This protocol — inferring the existence of an invisible structure by comparing two different conserved-quantity measurements — is the standard approach for detecting ker(F) structures across all domains of physics and beyond.
